# Harness design decides AI game quality

The games this platform should measure itself against (Hades, Stardew Valley, Celeste, Dead Cells, Hollow Knight, Balatro and a dozen others) were built on at least six different engines by teams of one to about twenty people, and what they share is not technology but discipline: one resolution, one palette, one animation method, exact feel constants, and years of post-launch iteration. That finding reframes the harness question. The runtime should be **Phaser 4** (345 KB gzipped, MIT, a headless mode and 27 shipped agent skills) wrapped in a thin platform SDK, but the quality lever is everything around the code generator: a vetted template library of pre-tested systems with Celeste-class feel presets baked in, a machine-readable style contract that every asset is quantized against, solver-validated levels, a mandatory test seam that lets Playwright bots and a Stop hook refuse any build that crashes, regresses or ships a hit with no feedback, and a community loop that ranks games by per-user engagement rather than raw plays. The "reference a game for style" feature should resolve a named title into a visible card of unprotectable attributes (genre, loop, camera, palette family, tone, feel preset), because US courts protect a game's audiovisual expression but not its rules, and trademark exposure lives in public metadata rather than private prompts. Every shipping prompt-to-game product (Rosebud, Astrocade, Google Playground) is chat-driven code generation on a fixed runtime with no published verification loop, and their reported failures (prototype-grade code, unverified feel, rule changes that break the game) map one-to-one onto the gates they lack. A fully generated game plausibly costs **$20–30 in API spend** with frontier models everywhere and under **$10** with model routing and templates, plus a few dollars of image and audio generation. The phased plan below reaches a gated single-genre vertical slice in about three months and a multiplayer, exportable community platform in roughly a year.

Unity, GameMaker, Godot, Unreal, Phaser and hand-rolled engines all appear on this list with equally acclaimed results, which is the first structural finding: **engine choice does not predict perceived quality**. What predicts it is a single strong art owner enforcing a tightly constrained style, and a willingness to keep shipping. The long-tail leaders (Stardew, Terraria, Dead Cells, Hollow Knight) earned most of their units from years of free content, not launch polish ([Gematsu](https://www.gematsu.com/2023/06/dead-cells-sales-top-10-million-further-updates-planned-through-2025)). The second structural finding is that the canon sorts into five art families that a style picker can name without naming any IP: 8-bit retro (NES palette, hard rules), 16-bit low-res pixel (320x180 canvas, 16–32 px sprites), HD pixel art with modern lighting (larger sprites, normal maps, particles, often rendered from 3D), painted or hand-drawn HD 2D (no pixel grid), and HD-2D diorama (pixel sprites in lit 3D scenes with depth of field). The 320x180 canvas matters because it scales by integer multiples to 1080p and 4K while 4:3 bases shimmer on 16:9 displays; a 2D project should fix its resolution before any art is drawn ([bugnet.io](https://bugnet.io/blog/choosing-a-pixel-art-resolution-for-your-game)). Celeste renders at a constant 320x180 and its Pico-8 prototype used a 16-colour palette the developers describe as "cleverly chosen artistically" ([Steam Community](https://steamcommunity.com/app/504230/discussions/0/3832043350345268297); [CCC transcript](https://static.media.ccc.de/media/conferences/camp2023/camp2023-57155-eng-Celeste_opus.txt)). Shovel Knight stuck to the NES palette plus four extra colours, removed sprite flicker, limited onscreen objects and particles on purpose, and tested every decision against whether it still "felt like an NES game" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/breaking-the-nes-for-shovel-knight)). Dead Cells' sole first-year artist modelled characters in 3DS Max and batch-rendered them to pixel sprites so retakes never required redrawing frames, then hand-painted backgrounds in Photoshop and gave them normal maps so one asset set could be relit per biome ([Habr](https://habr.com/post/416589); [80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/interview-with-the-developers-of-dead-cells)). Cuphead separates its 24 fps animation clock from its 60 fps simulation clock ([Unity](https://unity.com/made-with-unity/cuphead)). Octopath's team documented the two failure modes of its own style: not enough depth was "uninteresting", and too much resolution and saturation "lost the appeal of pixel art" ([Siliconera](https://www.siliconera.com/project-octopath-traveler-developers-answer-project-started-troubles-developing-hd-2d/)).

### Celeste's public source supplies the only developer-authored feel numbers

Game feel is the dimension prompt-to-game tools most visibly get wrong, and Celeste's open-sourced `Player.cs` is the single best primary artifact for fixing it, because it contains the exact constants the developers shipped. At 320x180 and 60 Hz the player runs at **90 px/s** with 1000 px/s² acceleration and 65% of that in the air; gravity is 900 with a half-gravity apex while the jump is held and vertical speed is under 40; jump speed is -105 with a 40 px/s horizontal boost; variable jump height lasts **0.2 s**; coyote time (`JumpGraceTime`) is **0.1 s**, six frames; wall jumps register 3 px from a wall; the dash runs at 240 px/s for 0.15 s with a 0.2 s cooldown and a **0.05 s freeze frame** on start; corner correction nudges the player 4 px around ceilings and dash obstacles; jumps squash the sprite to (0.6, 1.4), landings squash it up to (1.6, 0.4) scaled by fall speed and recover at 1.75 per second; a dash kicks the camera directionally for 0.2 s and fires a 0.4 s screen-distortion burst ([Player.cs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NoelFB/Celeste/master/Source/Player/Player.cs)). Maddy Thorson's own description of jump buffering ("if you press and hold the jump button a short time before landing, you will jump on the exact frame that you land") confirms these are deliberate forgiveness mechanics rather than accidents, and that they are standard rather than invented ([Celeste & Forgiveness](https://maddymakesgames.com/articles/celeste_and_forgiveness/index.html); [GameSpot](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/celeste-dev-explains-how-they-made-their-game-feel/1100-6474775/)). No equivalent numbers were published for Hades, Dead Cells or Hollow Knight, so for combat the harness has to rely on convergent third-party guidance: hitstop of 2–3 frames for jabs, 4–6 for standard hits and 8–10 for heavy blows ([MCO guide](https://mocaponline.com/blogs/mocap-news/animation-polish-game-feel-guide)), a controlled study placing optimum hitstop at **0.1–0.2 s** and optimum slow-motion at 0.2–0.4 s ([IEICE](https://ken.ieice.org/ken/paper/20230315OCSt/eng/)), freezing only attacker and target rather than the global timescale ([uhiyama-lab](https://uhiyama-lab.com/en/notes/godot/hit-feedback-hitstop/)), and Squirrel Eiserloh's trauma model for screen shake (a 0–1 trauma value decaying linearly, shake proportional to trauma squared or cubed, offsets driven by noise rather than random, applied only to the render transform) ([Bevy example](https://bevy.org/examples/camera/2d-screen-shake/)). Vlambeer's screenshake talk adds muzzle flash, camera lerp, hit pause, knockback, recoil and permanence to the checklist ([DK Liao devlog](https://dkliao.itch.io/the-art-of-screenshake-recreation/devlog/451576/quick-breakdown-of-all-the-effects)). The practical consequence is that a generator should ship a **"Celeste-class" preset** as data (60 Hz fixed step, 0.1 s coyote, ~0.1 s buffer, 0.2 s variable jump, apex half-gravity, 65% air control, 4 px corner correction, 0.05 s dash freeze, jump stretch (0.6, 1.4)) and a melee preset (3–5 frame light / 8–12 frame heavy hitstop plus trauma shake), and make them the default rather than something the model must rediscover.

### "Cheap" is a cohesion failure, not a budget failure

Postmortems converge on a diagnosis that is good news for an automated pipeline: games look amateurish because of inconsistency, which a machine can enforce, not because of low spend, which it cannot fix. A failed Kickstarter mixed cartoonish and realistic art from different creators, which "made the project look unfinished and cost it credibility"; mixing PBR assets with unlit low-poly ones produces a "Frankenstein's game"; teams keep placeholder art because they are invested in it ([Wayline](https://www.wayline.io/blog/free-art-fatal-kickstarter-failure); [Wayline](https://www.wayline.io/blog/frankensteins-game-free-assets-kill-sales); [Wayline](https://www.wayline.io/blog/looks-done-isnt-done-polish-first-mistake)). The fix is "art direction beats art budget": a short style guide, a fixed palette and one visual language early ([bugnet.io](https://bugnet.io/blog/art-direction-beats-art-budget)). Pixel-art communities name the most common sin "mixels", sprites at different pixel densities that make a game look "like all the sprites were downloaded from different sites", usually caused by non-integer scaling in the engine ([Flowlab forum](https://community.flowlab.io/t/mini-rant-stop-using-mixels/38287)). A deliberately lo-fi but consistent style (Undertale, Vampire Survivors) reads as intentional; a mismatched high-detail one reads as unfinished. For the harness, this means quality is mostly a set of **invariants to lint**: one base resolution, integer-only scaling, one global palette (or one per biome produced by lighting), one outline rule, one animation frame rate, one font family, and a rejection step for any asset that violates pixel density.

On which genres to generate first, the economics and the tractability point the same way. Action roguelikes and metroidvanias average $8.4M and $7.9M lifetime per Steam game but almost all revenue comes from a few hits ([GameDev Reports](https://gamedevreports.substack.com/p/weekly-gaming-reports-recap-july-8d7)); roguelike deckbuilders were the most successful indie tag by median with only 99 released from 2019 to 2022 ([WN Hub](https://wnhub.io/news/analytics/item-4582)); roguelite revenue on Steam reached an estimated **$400M in 2025**, up 80% ([Recognizing Patterns](https://recognizingpatterns.substack.com/p/the-roguelite-cull-hasnt-happened)). Survivors-likes and deckbuilders are systems-heavy and content-light per asset, precision platformers are tractable in art and level data provided the feel layer is exact, top-down farming sims are easy in art (16 px tiles, few frames) but heavy in scripting, and Hades- or Dead-Cells-class action games are hardest because they demand large animated character sets and bespoke bosses. The launch genres should therefore be the **precision platformer** (the feel preset and A*-solver validation already exist) and the **survivors-like arena** (the cheapest route to a game that feels complete), with top-down life sim and deckbuilder next and action roguelike last.

## Style references must resolve to cards of unprotectable attributes

The legal line for "in the style of X" is settled enough to design around. In Tetris Holding v. Xio Interactive the court held that there is no copyright in a game's rules or mechanics but that Tetris was protected in "the way in which it chose to express these game mechanics in the form of the audio-visual features of the game", and granted summary judgment on both copyright and trade dress because Xio had copied arbitrary choices (colours, board dimensions, piece shapes and motion) not required by the rules ([Proskauer](https://newmedialaw.proskauer.com/2012/06/19/videogame-app-developer-breaks-the-rules-on-copyright-infringement/); [Stones Law](https://www.stoneslaw.net/cloning-video-games-is-copyright-infringement)). Spry Fox v. LOLApps applied the same logic to Triple Town's object progression and characters, and the clone's IP was transferred to Spry Fox in settlement ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spry_Fox,_LLC_v._Lolapps,_Inc.)). "A farming sim with a day cycle, relationships and 16-bit pixel art" is therefore on the safe side; Stardew's sprites, portraits, map, names, UI chrome or logo are not. The next frontier is patents on mechanics: Nintendo's Palworld suit is a patent case over creature capture and riding, not a copyright claim, and after Pocketpair patched the mechanics the plaintiffs narrowed to older versions with analysts estimating roughly **$30K** in recoverable damages ([VGC](https://videogameschronicle.com/news/nintendo-and-the-pokemon-company-seek-an-injuction-and-damages-from-palworld-developer-pocketpair); [GamesFray](https://gamesfray.com/ahead-of-october-1-court-hearing-nintendo-has-zero-chance-of-prevailing-over-current-palworld-versions-it-may-get-30k-chump-change/)). Nintendo and Take-Two nonetheless treat UGC platforms as enforcement targets: 60+ DMCA notices against a mod site in a single month, a Super Mario creation removed from Dreams at Nintendo's request, and GTA mod takedowns whenever "the economy is threatened" ([GoNintendo](https://www.gonintendo.com/contents/36616-nintendo-sends-out-wave-of-dmca-takedowns-for-game-mods); [Nintendo Life](https://www.nintendolife.com/news/2020/03/sony_intends_to_remove_more_nintendo_projects_from_its_ps4_exclusive_dreams); [NME](https://www.nme.com/news/take-two-claims-it-is-pretty-flexible-on-removing-gta-mods-3009849)). Trademark exposure is a separate axis and arises mainly from marketing and discoverability: Nintendo's 2025 suit against Genki was about using the "Switch" name and designs publicly, not about any private use ([VGC](https://www.videogameschronicle.com/news/nintendo-is-suing-the-accessory-manufacturer-that-showed-off-switch-2-early)).

The generative-AI vendors have already converged on the operational version of this line. Gemini generated copyrighted characters freely in March 2025, received a Disney cease-and-desist in December, and by early 2026 refuses Disney characters while still honouring style requests ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/?p=2981172); [Android Authority](https://www.androidauthority.com/google-gemini-blocks-disney-generation-3639914)). OpenAI launched Sora 2 with rights-holder opt-out and reversed to **opt-in within about three days** ([eWeek](https://www.eweek.com/news/openai-sora-2-copyright-opt-out/)). Midjourney relies on ToS prohibitions and DMCA and is being sued by Disney, Universal and Warner over character outputs ([LA Mag](https://lamag.com/news/disney-and-universal-sue-ai-company-midjourney-for-copyright-infringement/)). The industry norm is thus "style and genre words allowed, specific characters and marks blocked for litigious rights-holders, licensing for everything else". No game-generation vendor publishes a named-game prompt policy, and none shows users what was extracted from a reference, which makes transparency a differentiator rather than a copy of existing practice. The Copyright Office's January 2025 report adds a second reason for transparency: "prompts alone do not provide sufficient human control" for authorship, while human selection, arrangement and modification can be protected, and the D.C. Circuit affirmed in Thaler v. Perlmutter that a machine cannot be an author ([IPWatchdog](https://ipwatchdog.com/2025/01/29/part-two-copyright-office-ai-report-says-creative-prompting-doesnt-constitute-authorship/); [Reed Smith](https://www.reedsmith.com/our-insights/blogs/viewpoints/102k53m/dc-circuit-affirms-ai-cannot-be-author-under-copyright-act/)). A user who edits a visible style card is exercising exactly the kind of creative control that report describes.

The recommended design is a deterministic **reference resolver** feeding a **style card**. The platform maintains a curated catalog of roughly 500–2,000 well-known games keyed by title and aliases; each entry maps to a card containing only unprotectable attributes: genre and sub-genre, core loop (for example "daily cycle, tend plots, sell, upgrade"), session structure, progression model, camera and perspective, art family and canvas size, animation style, palette descriptor, lighting mood, tone, pacing, difficulty posture, music mood, UI feel, and a short list of generic mechanics such as "dash with invulnerability frames" or "coyote time", never the reference's proper nouns. Blocked attributes are never copied: character names and designs, place and item names, logos and wordmarks, brand-identifying fonts, story and dialogue, specific level layouts, music, distinctive UI layouts, and the arbitrary visual choices the Tetris court protected. The named title is a private lookup key only; it never appears in public titles, tags, store pages or in any prompt sent to an image or music model. For unknown titles, an LLM extraction into the same schema runs with the blocked-attribute filter applied and the result is surfaced for confirmation. The card is shown to the user ("here is what I took from your reference: top-down farming loop, 16-bit pixel art, cozy tone, forgiving difficulty") with an edit affordance, which documents human control, creates an audit trail that the platform never instructed the model to reproduce expression, and improves generation by turning a fuzzy reference into explicit parameters. Asset outputs are screened before publication with embedding similarity against a set of famous characters and logos plus OCR for wordmarks, with re-rolls on hits. For enforcement, mirror Epic's model, where each unresolved infringement earns an account strike and only rights-holders can file ([Fortnite IP guidelines](https://www.fortnite.com/news/intellectual-property-ip-and-dmca-guidelines-for-fortnite-island-creators)), and Roblox's IP Platform, where a rights-holder can offer the creator a licence instead of demanding removal, noting Roblox's own observation that "the majority of complaints we see about copies of Roblox-created items do not in fact infringe" ([Roblox IP Platform Terms](https://en.help.roblox.com/hc/en-us/articles/21857240911380-IP-Platform-Terms-of-Use)). Licensed "official" style packs on the Epic–LEGO model, where brand rules forbid mixing with other IP and the rights-holder takes a revenue share ([LEGO Brand Rules](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/fortnite/lego-brand-and-creator-rules-in-fortnite-creative)), are the only safe way to ever let a user literally say "make a Mario level".

Genie's prompt guide offers an independent sanity check on what a card should contain: DeepMind frames prompts as environment plus controllable character plus world sketch, and practitioners report that palette, camera angle and texture cues plus one or two mechanical verbs work best ([DeepMind](https://deepmind.google/models/genie/prompt-guide/); [Macaron](https://macaron.im/blog/genie-3-capabilities-limitations)). Those are precisely the fields above.

## Phaser 4 in a thin SDK beats every alternative for instant-play, remixable games

The engine decision for a browser-first, LLM-authored, remixable platform is driven by bundle size, API stability relative to training data, first-party agent documentation, headless testability and whether the runtime can live inside a sandboxed iframe, not by raw renderer speed, which every JavaScript option has in surplus for 2D. Phaser 4.0 "Caladan" shipped on 10 April 2026 as the framework's biggest release with a new renderer, filters and lighting ([phaser.io](https://phaser.io/download/stable); [gamedev.net](https://gamedev.net/news/2759-phaser-4-released/)); the current 4.2.1 package measures **1,344 KB raw / 344 KB gzip** for the full build and 312 KB gzip for the Arcade-physics build, ships bundled TypeScript definitions, a `HEADLESS` render type, Arcade and Matter physics, tilemaps, particles, tweens, cameras, a unified Filter system (blur, glow, bloom, pixelate, colour matrix, image-based lighting, quantize) and a `SpriteGPULayer` for very large sprite counts, all under MIT with a commercial maintainer ([unpkg README](https://unpkg.com/phaser@4.2.1/README.md); [npm](https://www.npmjs.com/package/phaser)). Decisively for this use case, the 4.2.1 tarball contains **27 `SKILL.md` agent skills** with front-matter triggers, including a v3-to-v4 migration skill that directly addresses the drift between the model's training data (mostly Phaser 3) and the shipped API, plus eight reference files, and the README states that "Phaser's API is well understood by every major frontier LLM" ([unpkg README](https://unpkg.com/phaser@4.2.1/README.md)). Rosebud, the closest existing precedent, runs all of its 2D games on Phaser ([phaser.io](https://phaser.io/news/2024/01/create-phaser-games-from-prompts-with-rosebud-ai)), and Vampire Survivors shipped on Phaser until its v1.6 engine switch ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vampire_Survivors)). The renderer is WebGL1 with WebGL2 features polyfilled through extensions, a compatibility choice for Safari rather than a performance ceiling ([phaser.io devlog](https://www.phaser.io/devlogs/235)).

Godot deserves a specific rejection because it is the engine desktop-oriented AI editors (Summer Engine, Orca) wrap. A default Godot 4 web export is about 42 MB and only a custom-built export template with Brotli reaches ~2.7 MB, still roughly eight times Phaser's entire bundle, with size "mostly unchanged in 4.5" ([popcar](https://popcar.bearblog.dev/how-to-minify-godots-build-size/)); threaded builds additionally require cross-origin isolation headers that conflict with iframe embedding ([Godot docs](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/4.2/tutorials/export/exporting_for_web.html)). Phaser's own April 2026 comparison against KAPLAY and Excalibur (a vendor source, so treat the ranking as interested) adds that only Phaser supports headless mode for server-side simulation and claims AI tools hallucinate less for Phaser ([phaser.io](https://phaser.io/news/2026/04/phaser-vs-kaplay-vs-excalibur-2d-web-game-framework)); `@geckos.io/phaser-on-nodejs` runs Phaser under Node with jsdom for Arcade or Matter simulation in CI or on a server ([npm](https://www.npmjs.com/package/@geckos.io/phaser-on-nodejs)). For physics, Arcade (AABB) is the default for platformers and survivors-likes; Matter is bundled but its upstream has not published since June 2024 ([npm](https://www.npmjs.com/package/matter-js)); the TypeScript Box2D port `planck` at 54 KB gzip is the lightest actively maintained rigid-body option if Matter proves insufficient, while Rapier's ~0.9–1.3 MB of WASM is too heavy for instant play ([npm](https://www.npmjs.com/package/planck); [npm](https://www.npmjs.com/package/@dimforge/rapier2d-compat)).

The important design move is not to pick the smallest library but to **wrap Phaser in a thin platform SDK** that gives KAPLAY-like simplicity with Phaser's stability: one fixed scene template, a fixed-timestep update driven by an injectable clock, a seeded PRNG in place of `Math.random`, an event bus every gameplay system publishes to, and the test hooks described below. Pin one Phaser 4.x minor, inject the matching shipped skills into the agent's context, and run an in-house evaluation (same prompt set across Phaser, KAPLAY and Pixi plus ECS, scored by Playwright pass rate) before locking in, since every claim about per-engine LLM accuracy currently available is qualitative or vendor-authored.

## The harness should fix the runtime, template the systems and gate every build

Every shipping prompt-to-game product that discloses anything is a chat-driven code generator on a fixed runtime paired with generative asset models, and none publishes its pipeline. Rosebud's flow is a 500-character prompt into a chat with its agent "Rosie", a live preview and asset management, with an uploaded image accepted as a visual reference ([Rosebud](https://lab.rosebud.ai/blog/rosebud-ai-interface-guide); [nilo.io](https://www.nilo.io/articles/how-rosebud-ai-works)). Astrocade's relaunch added a brainstorming step that turns half-formed concepts into "rich game blueprints" and then "generates a customized edit mode" per game, orchestrated by a proprietary multi-model agent; it reports **10 million monthly users** and a $56M Series B, and its CEO concedes AI "can't actually build a great game all on its own" ([NatLawReview](https://natlawreview.com/press-releases/astrocade-rolls-out-ai-agent-powered-game-creation-experience-opening-door); [Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/ai-generated-games-platform-already-has-10-million-monthly-users-2000741894); [Fortune](https://www.fortune.com/2026/05/05/astrocade-raises-56-million-series-b-sequoia-video-games-platform-ali-amir-sadeghian/)). Google's Playground, launched 7 October 2026, runs "Gemini, Nano Banana, and Lyria models inside a custom harness", and launch-week hands-ons call the results "rough" ([AI Weekly](https://aiweekly.co/alerts/google-launches-playground-a-no-code-browser-game-maker-powered-by-gemini-nano); [TweakTown](https://www.tweaktown.com/news/113985/anyone-can-create-ai-games-with-googles-new-playground-tool/index.html)). The criticisms are consistent: a tester of Rosebud output found the code redundant and uncommented with debugging slower than writing by hand, concluding it is "a prototyping accelerator rather than a game factory" ([CSDN summary](https://gitcode.csdn.net/69d4f0b854b52172bc6788c8.html)); a head-to-head of general app builders concluded that "a polished screen does not prove that the full game loop works" ([AI Fire](https://www.aifire.co/p/ai-game-builders-tested-lovable-vs-base44-vs-replit)); commentators on Playground say the real test is "whether controls feel right and whether changing one rule breaks the rest of a game" ([NerdZap](https://nerdzap.com/news/google-playground-ai-game-maker-uk-roblox-build/)). Each of those complaints is a missing harness feature: tests, regression gates, deterministic templates, playtest bots, a feel checklist. Google's choice of the word "harness" to describe its own product confirms where vendors believe the work is.

The research literature supplies the recipe. AutoUE, the most complete agentic game-generation system published (ACL Findings 2026), coordinates separate agents for asset retrieval, scene generation, code synthesis, interaction and play-testing, grounds generation in engine documentation, and its ablations show that **predefined patterns, documentation-grounded retrieval and engine-specific code constraints each matter** ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07106); [EmergentMind](https://www.emergentmind.com/papers/2603.07106)). LLM level generation is viable but not self-validating: MarioGPT produces playable levels about **88%** of the time as judged by an A* agent, versus ~31% for the best baseline, and Sokoban generators rely on a tree-search solver with a step budget to decide solvability ([MarioGPT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.05981); [Todd et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.05817)). LLMs are weak at exactly the skills playtesting needs: on gg-bench GPT-4o and Claude 3.7 Sonnet win only **7–9%** of games in-context and reasoning models 31–36%, failing at multi-turn strategy and spatial reasoning ([gg-bench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.07215)). Anthropic's harness guidance states the governing principle directly: give the agent "something it can run that comes back pass or fail", keep CLAUDE.md under about 200 lines holding only what cannot be read from the repo, move procedures into progressively disclosed skills, enforce non-negotiables with deterministic hooks rather than emphasis ("if you keep adding emphasis to a CLAUDE.md line and Claude still misses it, that's a sign the line belongs in a hook"), use a Stop hook that exits 2 to keep the agent working until typecheck passes, and use subagents for fresh-eyes review rather than for better answers ([Claude Code best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices); [Hooks docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/hooks)). Skills are directories whose YAML description is preloaded while the body and bundled scripts load only when relevant, and LLMs are costlier than code for deterministic work, so scripts belong in skills ([Anthropic engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/equipping-agents-for-the-real-world-with-agent-skills)). The verification hierarchy is rules-based first (TypeScript with linting "gives more feedback than plain JavaScript"), visual screenshots second, and LLM-as-judge last as "generally less robust and costly in latency" ([Agent SDK post](https://claude.com/blog/building-agents-with-the-claude-agent-sdk)). A secondary report of an AI Engineer Summit talk claims the same Opus 4.5 scored 78% on a benchmark under Claude Code's harness versus 42% under another framework, which, if accurate, quantifies how much the harness matters ([ML6](https://www.ml6.eu/en/blog/inside-the-claude-agents-sdk-lessons-from-the-ai-engineer-summit)).

Putting those together, the pipeline splits cleanly into what the LLM does (design, selection, authoring, critique) and what code does (scaffolding, validation, solving, diffing, budgeting, gating).

| Stage | Actor | Output and gate |
|---|---|---|
| Intake interview ("interview me until you could write the spec") | frontier LLM, interactive | `concept.md`; style card resolved from any referenced games |
| GDD with three pillars, core loop at 30 s / 5 min / session, verbs, progression, scope budget | LLM from a skill template | `gdd.md`; deterministic schema check on required sections and numeric scope |
| System selection from a vetted catalog (movement, combat, inventory, dialogue, progression, save, HUD, camera, audio bus, input remap, accessibility) | LLM selects and parameterises; code scaffolds | `systems.json`; scaffold generator emits engine boilerplate, typed config, tests, CI |
| Glue and bespoke mechanics against typed interfaces | LLM in TypeScript | PostToolUse prettier/eslint; PreToolUse blocks edits inside `templates/` internals; reviewer subagent checks against `gdd.md`, "skip style" |
| Levels as data with a difficulty ramp spec | LLM proposes | BFS/A* validator with the movement model's jump arc checks reachability, win path, spawn safety, collectible counts, difficulty metrics; regenerate on failure |
| Assets under the style card | image/audio models; code post-processes | downscale, grid-snap, palette quantize, alpha cleanup, atlas pack, loudness normalise; reject on palette or size violation |
| Automated playtest | code first, LLM second | headless seeded run; scripted bots per system; coverage exploration; then a bounded VLM critique with a validated rubric |
| Polish/juice gate | deterministic lint plus an LLM tuning pass | every GDD feedback event binds at least one feedback channel; coyote/buffer present; clamps on shake amplitude and hitstop |
| Performance and size budgets | deterministic | p95 frame time under CPU throttle, bundle and asset bytes, load time, memory growth; Stop hook fails over budget |
| Publish | deterministic | static bundle, thumbnail from a validated screenshot, `gdd.md` / `systems.json` / `style.json` / playtest report stored for later iteration |

The repository that implements this follows the Claude Code conventions literally. `CLAUDE.md` stays under 200 lines and holds only commands (`pnpm test`, `pnpm typecheck`, `pnpm playtest --seed`, `pnpm budget`), gotchas (the `?test=1` seam, the `window.__TEST__.ready` signal) and decisions (TypeScript only, fixed runtime, never edit template internals). `.claude/rules/` carries path-scoped conventions (`src/systems/**`, `content/levels/**` as data that must pass the validator, `assets/**` with palette and filtering rules, `ui/**`), which load only where relevant ([best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)). `.claude/skills/` holds the procedures with bundled scripts: `gdd-author`, `systems-select` with the catalog and scaffold script, `level-design` with the generator and `validate_level`, `asset-prompting` with the style-card composer and post-processors, `playtest` with the bot runner, `juice-pass` with the feel checklist and clamps, `budget-check`, `publish` (marked `disable-model-invocation`) and a recorded `verify` recipe. `.claude/agents/` defines a read-only `gdd-reviewer` on a mid-tier model, a `playtester` on the cheapest adequate model, an `art-director` vision critic whose rubric is generated from the style card, and a Haiku-class `edit-router`. Hooks in `settings.json` run formatters on every edit, block protected paths, inject the current GDD summary and last playtest report at session start, and make the Stop hook run `tsc && vitest && playtest --smoke && budget-check`, exiting 2 on failure so an unattended generation cannot declare itself done with a broken build ([Hooks docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/hooks)).

User iteration is where this design pays off most. A small model classifies every edit request as a parameter tweak (change `systems.json` or `style.json`, regenerate deterministically with no code edit), a content edit (regenerate the level or dialogue and re-validate) or a code change (full loop with regression gates). Each accepted build is a checkpoint users can rewind to, and each build ships with evidence: the bot report, a screenshot grid and the budget table. This is what turns "changing one rule breaks the rest of the game" from a complaint into a test that fails before the user sees it.

The style card is the interface between all of this and the asset pipeline. Encoded as `style.json`, it carries logical resolution and integer scale factor, pixel-art flag and filtering, an ordered palette with named roles (background, mid, foreground, accent, danger, UI), outline rule, per-category animation frame rates, camera parameters (follow type, deadzone, lerp, maximum shake, zoom), typography (bitmap font, sizes), UI tokens, audio mood words, post-processing (vignette, bloom, CRT), reference images with one-line notes on what to take from each, and a negative list. It is placed first in every asset prompt so the stable prefix hits the prompt cache (cache reads cost roughly 5% of input price on current Claude models ([Claude pricing](https://claude.com/pricing))), it is enforced by the deterministic validators, it is restated in `.claude/rules/assets.md` only in code-relevant terms, and it generates the VLM rubric so judgments are relative to the declared style rather than the model's taste. No vendor or paper documents an internal style representation, so this card is a design synthesis rather than a precedent, but every one of its properties follows from a sourced harness principle.

## Assets need a style contract, grid-native generators and a deterministic de-AI pass

The asset pipeline is where "typical prompt-to-game output" is most visibly cheap, and the 2026 tooling splits into two classes that the harness must treat differently. Only a small set of purpose-built tools generate on a true pixel grid natively: a 2026 comparison names PixelLab, Retro Diffusion, Sprixen and Sprite-AI as grid-native, while Ludo, Scenario, Layer and Leonardo "can be steered toward it by prompts and usually need a downscaling step", and ranks PixelLab best for animated characters because it is "the only tool it reviewed that builds sprites from a skeleton you can pose" ([Cinevva](https://app.cinevva.com/guides/ai-pixel-art-generators)). PixelLab's Rotate tool produces eight directional views at 16, 32, 64 or 128 px canvases, its skeleton workflow estimates a rig from a sprite and poses it per frame, and its API exposes persisted characters with eight directions to which animations are attached as background jobs, which is the closest thing to a "character object" a code pipeline can drive deterministically ([PixelLab docs](https://www.pixellab.ai/docs/tools/rotate); [PixelLab docs](https://www.pixellab.ai/docs/tools/skeleton-animation); [oomol listing](https://oomol.com/es/skills/oo-pixellab/source.md)). Retro Diffusion was "trained in collaboration with experienced pixel artists", generates on a grid at native sizes up to 256x256, offers seamless `tileX`/`tileY` wrapping, and its RD Tile model builds Wang-combination tilesets and material transitions ([Scenario](https://help.scenario.com/en/articles/retro-diffusion-models-the-essentials/); [Scenario docs](https://docs.scenario.com/docs/third-party-model-generation-retro-diffusion)); its partner blog explains why general diffusion fails at this ("varying pixel sizes, inconsistent outlines, blurry effects, and random noise patterns" are "fundamental to how AI image generation works") ([Runware](https://runware.ai/blog/retro-diffusion-creating-authentic-pixel-art-with-ai-at-scale)). PixelLab's tileset tool exports Wang, dual-grid 15-tile and 3x3 formats ([PixelLab docs](https://www.pixellab.ai/docs/tools/create-tileset)). Pricing is poorly documented: third-party listings put Retro Diffusion at roughly **$0.015–0.18 per image** on prepaid credits and PixelLab at $10–50 per month in tiers whose names and caps differ across sources ([mcpservers.org](https://mcpservers.org/servers/retro-diffusion/retro-diffusion-mcp); [Knowara](https://knowara.com/ai-tools/image/pixellab-review/)); both need confirmation against official pages before budgeting.

General models remain the right choice for painted, hand-drawn and HD-2D families, with two rules. First, never ask for a full sprite grid in one prompt: a three-week independent test of gpt-image-2 found that "asking for a full grid of poses in one prompt failed every single time", with mirrored limbs and inconsistent proportions between cells ([dredyson](https://dredyson.com/?p=87763)). Second, condition on a reference: generate a front view first and derive side and back views from it, as Rosebud's own Nano Banana guide and Scenario's 8-direction app do ([Rosebud](https://rosebud.ai/blog/how-to-create-a-sprite-sheet-with-ai-using-google-gemini-and-nano-banana-easy-guide); [Scenario](https://help.scenario.com/articles/2974691727-scenario-apps-for-gaming)). Costs here are concrete: Nano Banana at about **$0.039 per image**, Nano Banana Pro at ~$0.134, gpt-image-2 from ~$0.006 (low) to $0.21 (high) per 1024-px image, Scenario's API from $0.004 per image ([cloudprice](https://cloudprice.net/models/google/nano-banana-pro); [aireiter](https://aireiter.com/blog/gpt-image-2-api-pricing); [Scenario](https://docs.scenario.com/page/api-pricing)). Flux pixel-art LoRAs are tempting but at least one is explicitly non-commercial under FLUX.1 [dev] terms ([Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/deAPI-ai/pixelartdotfluxlora/blob/main/README.md)), and Leonardo's free tier outputs are public and non-commercial ([Ludo](https://ludo.ai/compare/leonardo-alternatives)), so the pipeline must block free-tier and non-commercial sources by construction.

Every raster output then goes through a deterministic **de-AI pass**, because the tells are mechanical and therefore fixable. Pixel-art communities identify AI work by outline pixels of visibly different sizes, misaligned grids, intermediate colours and anti-aliasing, and "an excessive number of unique colors" ([r/PixelArt](https://redlib.ssps.io/r/PixelArt/comments/1k26vck/i_dont_use_ai/mnseit0/?context=3); [Gigazine](https://gigazine.net/news/20260516-spritefusion-pixel-snapper/); [Fiverr](https://fiverr.com/mothblank/fix-your-ai-generated-pixel-art)). Sprite Fusion's Pixel Snapper, open source in Rust and self-hostable, "makes pixels uniform and snaps them to a grid" with a palette-size slider ([Gigazine](https://gigazine.net/news/20260516-spritefusion-pixel-snapper/)); ImageMagick remaps to a palette image with `+dither -remap` (dithering is on by default and must be disabled) ([gendev forum](https://gendev.spritesmind.net/forum/viewtopic.php?p=20171)); Aseprite runs headless with `-b --script` for batch operations ([Aseprite CLI](https://www.aseprite.org/cli/)). The pass is: PNG only, nearest-neighbour downscale to the contract canvas, grid snap, palette remap, then QA checks (unique colours at or below palette size, uniform pixel size, clean alpha) with rejection and regeneration on failure, and integer nearest-neighbour upscaling only at render time. Palette discipline follows community norms of **4–16 colours** with three to five values per hue from Lospec-style palettes ([PixelJoint](https://pixeljoint.com/forum/forum_posts.asp?TID=26679); [Pixnote](https://pixnote.net/en/learn/glossary)). Dead Cells' trick of normal-mapped backgrounds lit at runtime is the cheapest way to make one asset set read as many biomes and to homogenise mixed-source assets ([80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/case-study-dead-cells-character-art-pipeline/)); Phaser 4's image-based lighting filter makes that practical in the browser.

Animation should stay frame-based and low-count for pixel families: AI inbetweening for hand-drawn frames is judged "not there yet" by an independent practitioner ([yosefk](https://yosefk.com/blog/the-state-of-ai-for-hand-drawn-animation-inbetweening.html)), Spine's maker says auto-rigging "is not yet on our roadmap" ([Spine forum](https://esotericsoftware.com/forum/d/24270-auto-rig-ai-)), and pixel art tolerates 4–8 frame cycles. Skeletal rigs earn their place for painted and HD-2D styles, where part-splitting tools (Layer's Spine component generator, an open-source Claude skill that uses Gemini to re-skin a rig without re-rigging) allow reskinning without regenerating frames ([Layer](https://layer.ai/tools/layer--create-spine-components); [GitHub](https://github.com/GenielabsOpenSource/spine-animation-ai)). UI chrome, particles and VFX should be **drawn in code**: a 2025 SBGames paper shows LLMs can emit "structurally valid and scalable" SVG game assets with an aesthetic gap only against proprietary models ([SBGames](https://sol.sbc.org.br/index.php/sbgames/article/view/45382)), and 9-slice panels, bars and icons need exact geometry a diffusion model cannot respect. Fonts should come from the permissively licensed set: Press Start 2P and Pixelify Sans under SIL OFL, m5x7 under CC0 ([FontLibrary](https://fontlibrary.org/en/font/press-start-2p); [itch.io](https://managore.itch.io/m5x7)).

Audio licensing is the least settled part of the pipeline and dictates the defaults. ElevenLabs Sound Effects generates 0.1–30 s clips with a looping mode at 40 credits per second ([ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/product/sound-effects/overview)), and ChipTone output is **CC0** for any purpose, which makes procedural chiptune the zero-risk fallback and the right default for the retro families ([ChipTone](https://sfbgames.itch.io/chiptone)). For music, Suno has no public API and its ToS prohibits automated use, with commercial rights tied to capped paid-plan downloads under terms effective 3 September 2026 ([Sonilo](https://sonilo.com/blog/guides/official-suno-api-saas-builders-2026); [Suno](https://suno.com/blog/suno-updates-tos)); Udio's downloads remained unavailable as of March 2026 after its label settlements ([Metavert](https://metavert.io/compare/udio-vs-elevenlabs)); ElevenLabs Music is built on licensed catalogs but third-party guides report that self-serve plans exclude "Studio Games", which must be confirmed before use ([Cinevva](https://app.cinevva.com/guides/ai-music-generators-games)). The programmatic options with explicit grants are Stable Audio 3.0 (open weights, user owns output, commercial use under the Community License for organisations under **$1M revenue**) and Google Lyria 3 at roughly **$0.04–0.08 per song** through the Gemini API with a SynthID watermark ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/stability-ai-launches-stable-audio-3-0-with-up-to-six-minute-tracks-and-open-weights/); [Stability](https://stability.ai/news/stable-audio-open-research-paper); [InVideo](https://invideo.io/blog/lyria-ai-music-generator/)). Every asset gets a provenance record (tool, model version, prompt, references, post-processing, human edits) because Steam requires it, because the Copyright Office position makes human edits the only basis for a copyright claim, and because the platform must pass each vendor's commercial grant through to the end user.

## Verification must be solver-first, bots second and vision judges last

The single most consequential harness requirement is that **every generated game exposes a test seam**. Practitioner skills converge on the same protocol: a single canvas, `window.render_game_to_text()` returning state as text, `window.advanceTime(ms)` for deterministic stepping, a ready flag such as `window.__TEST__.ready` to wait on instead of timeouts, Playwright's `page.clock.install()` and `runFor()` for frame-precise control, and a rule that the agent must actually open and inspect screenshots captured beyond the start screen ([OpenAI develop-web-game skill](https://skills.cat/skills/trailofbits/skills-curated/openai-develop-web-game); [game-qa skill](https://skills.cat/skills/opusgamelabs/game-creator/game-qa); [Phaser Playwright skill](https://agentskills.so/skills/chongdashu-phaserjs-oakwoods-playwright-testing)). The platform SDK should mandate a fixed-timestep loop with an injectable clock, a seeded PRNG, `window.__game.state()` and `window.__game.step(n)`, a replayable input log, and semantic event hooks (`onHit`, `onDeath`, `onLevelComplete`, `onPickup`). State-as-text is the primary oracle because headless WebGL via software rendering can diverge from GPU output, which is why the skills advise re-running headed when a capture looks wrong. This one requirement unlocks every metric below at near-zero marginal cost.

Playability and difficulty are the best-understood metrics. The PCG evaluation literature treats "whether or not a level can be completed" as the standard playability feature ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.18657)); A*-agent completion is the proven baseline for tile-based platformers ([MarioGPT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.05981)); search effort to solve a level is a usable difficulty proxy that correlates with existing estimates in some domains and not others ([Beukman et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.10334)); persona agents estimate spread across player types ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.02100)); and at Rovio, AI players' best-case runs correlated with human engagement and difficulty data better than their averages did ([Roohi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.12061v1)). LLM playtest agents have now crossed into production: Lap achieved higher code coverage and triggered more crashes than three existing tools on a match-3 game, and TITAN found four previously unknown bugs in commercial games and is "deployed in eight real-world game QA pipelines" ([Lap](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09490); [TITAN](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.22170)). Latency sensitivity follows Claypool's precision-and-deadline framework, in which high-precision, tight-deadline actions are most sensitive, which puts platformers and shoot-em-ups in the tightest budget class ([Claypool](https://sites.cs.ucsb.edu/~almeroth/classes/W10.290F/papers/claypool-10.pdf)). There is no industry standard for measuring game feel ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_feel)), so juice has to be instrumented as presence checks rather than perceived.

| Checklist item | Machine check in the harness |
|---|---|
| Coyote time (Celeste) | inject jump N frames after leaving a ledge; assert it fires for N up to ~5–6 at 60 Hz |
| Jump buffering (Celeste) | press jump M frames before landing; assert jump on the landing frame |
| Variable jump and apex float | short vs held jump heights differ; gravity scale reduced near apex |
| Hit pause (Vlambeer) | on `onHit`, time-scale dips or frames freeze for 1–6 frames, never more than ~100 ms |
| Screen shake | camera offset magnitude rises on hit and decays within 300 ms; shake always stops |
| Hit flash, knockback, particles | tint or flash lifecycle, velocity impulse on target, emitter burst on hit and death |
| Audio feedback | every core verb has an SFX event; no silent first 10 s; no SFX pile-ups per frame |
| Camera lerp and bounds | follow is smoothed, never 1:1; camera never leaves level bounds |
| Emphasise success | level-complete triggers a celebration sequence of at least 500 ms |
| Readability | contrast between player sprite and background above a threshold by image analysis |
| Input latency | key event to first velocity change and to first visible feedback measured in frames |
| Frame pacing and budgets | p95 `requestAnimationFrame` delta over a 60 s scripted run under CPU throttle; long tasks; memory growth |
| Difficulty curve | deaths per segment from bots; flag non-monotonic spikes; require a "can't fail" first 30 seconds |
| Feedback loop (Supergiant) | telemetry hooks for death position, stuck time and quit point, aggregated into a stuck heatmap |

Vision-language critique belongs last and bounded. The relevant literature is clear that a visual critic must reject regressions (ReLook gives invalid renders zero reward and admits "only improving revisions") and must guard against a local fix regressing other regions (RubSE) ([ReLook](https://dev.to/paperium/relook-vision-grounded-rl-with-a-multimodal-llm-critic-for-agentic-web-coding-4d0o); [RubSE](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.24138)); that vision models "struggle with self-generated critiques" ([VISCO](https://themoonlight.io/review/visco-benchmarking-fine-grained-critique-and-correction-towards-self-improvement-in-visual-reasoning)); and that LLM judges carry a dominant style bias (0.76–0.92 across five judges, preferring markdown over identical plain prose), recency bias and self-preference ([Judging the Judges](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.23178v1); [LLMs Judge Themselves](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.15746)). The ICLR 2026 study of language models' evaluations of 121 novel board games found moderate alignment with more than 450 human fun and fairness judgments, against a human split-half ceiling of R² = 0.82 ([paper note](https://en.papernotes.org/ICLR2026/llm_evaluation/evaluating_language_models_evaluations_of_games/)), which is encouraging for triage and insufficient for a gate. The workable design is a Haiku- or Sonnet-class vision pass over a 10–20 frame contact sheet with a rubric generated from the style card, using pairwise comparison against a reference set rather than absolute scores, randomised order, identical formatting, a different model family from the generator, validated once against roughly 50–80 hand-labelled screenshots (the Real-Time World Crafting paper validated its judge against 80 hand-labelled examples with paired tests ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.16952))), and used as advisory findings while deterministic screenshot diffs with masked dynamic regions gate regressions.

Community signals complete the loop and should follow Roblox, the only platform that documents its ranking. Roblox averages engagement, retention and monetisation signals per user "so smaller experiences with highly engaged users are not disadvantaged", prioritises play-through rate, play days per user, playtime per user capped at 60 minutes a day and first-play bounce rate, uses qualified play sessions to filter accidental clicks, and excludes traffic from ads, curation and search from ranking so that featuring does not self-reinforce ([Roblox Discovery](https://create.roblox.com/docs/discovery)). itch.io keeps its formulas secret to prevent gaming ([itch.io forum](https://itch.io/post/10563164)). The platform should adopt the per-user signal set, add remix count and remix depth (a remixed game that is itself remixed is a strong quality signal), completion rate of the generated tutorial segment, and a post-play rating with a mandatory one-tag reason (fun, confusing, broken, ugly) that doubles as training data for judge calibration, and use the automated gates as a pre-listing filter so broken games never enter the feed. For human playtesting, PlaytestCloud pays testers **$5–9 per 15-minute session** ([PlaytestCloud](https://players.playtestcloud.com/article/118-rewards-a-summary)), Supergiant read every upvoted Discord request while noting the most upvoted was not the most urgent and used anonymous stuck-point data and content in every update ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/uk/a-journey-through-early-access-helped-make-hades-a-masterpiece/)), and Rosebud runs monthly jams with prizes ([Discord listing](https://discord.com/servers/rosebud-ai-773712199729414144)). No UGC platform was found running a formal rewarded community-playtest loop, so a test queue of unreleased games with a structured 5–10 minute task, rewards in generation credits scaled by tester reliability, and tester labels fed back as ground truth for the judge is a genuine differentiator rather than table stakes.

## Multiplayer, sandboxing, policy and cost set the operating envelope

Multiplayer should be one pattern baked into every generated game: an **authoritative Colyseus room**. Colyseus is MIT, provides rooms, matchmaking and typed schema state sync, and its 0.18 SDK ships a drop-in prediction layer that smooths remote entities with lerp and dead reckoning and gives the local player "ACTIVE server-reconciled rollback" (apply input now, rewind to server truth and replay), with optimistic discrete events that confirm or auto-reject ([npm @colyseus/sdk](https://www.npmjs.com/package/@colyseus/sdk); [Colyseus 0.17 blog](https://colyseus.io/blog/colyseus-017-is-here/); [cinevva](https://app.cinevva.com/de/guides/multiplayer-browser-game)). Authoritative servers are the only realistic choice because they tolerate non-deterministic, buggy or cheating clients. The alternative, peer-to-peer rollback as in NetplayJS, requires a fully rewindable deterministic game ([npm](https://www.npmjs.com/package/netplayjs)), a constraint LLM-generated code violates silently through `Math.random`, `Date.now` or floating-point physics; even Rapier's standard builds "do not guarantee cross-platform determinism" ([rapier.rs](https://rapier.rs/docs/user_guides/javascript/determinism)). PlayroomKit is a reasonable zero-backend option for party games but its authority is an elected player host and its SDK weighs 353 KB gzipped ([npm](https://www.npmjs.com/package/playroomkit)). The harness ships a fixed room template (`GameRoom extends Room<State>` with `defineInput`, a 20 Hz tick, a players-and-entities schema) and matching client scene, runs the shared simulation module server-side via phaser-on-nodejs or a renderer-free sim module, and never asks the model to design netcode. Hosting substrates are interchangeable: PartyKit now runs on Cloudflare Durable Objects after its 2024 acquisition ([CB Insights](https://www.cbinsights.com/investor/partykit)), Rivet offers self-hostable actors with a free tier and metered pricing ([rivet.dev](https://rivet.dev/cloud/)), while Hathora was acquired in March 2026 and its client SDK has been dormant since 2023 ([CB Insights](https://www.cbinsights.com/company/hathora/financials)).

Sandboxing follows the documented pattern for agent-generated HTML: run each game in an iframe with `sandbox="allow-scripts"` and without `allow-same-origin` so it has an opaque origin and no access to the parent's DOM or storage, place a `<meta>` Content-Security-Policy first in the document so it applies before guest scripts and denies network and navigation, and expose platform APIs (assets, multiplayer tokens, state readback) over a MessageChannel bridge; pairing `allow-scripts` with `allow-same-origin` would let the frame remove its own sandbox ([agentpatterns.ai](https://www.agentpatterns.ai/security/browser-sandbox-agent-generated-html/); [curiosity.ai](https://docs.curiosity.ai/tesserae/components/sandbox)). This model is also what makes remixing cheap: each game is a self-contained bundle of a few kilobytes of code sharing one cached engine file, served from a per-game origin, so the platform never executes generated code in its own origin and generated code never holds credentials. Server-side execution of generated logic is needed only for authoritative multiplayer, and there the choice is between V8 isolates and containers. Cloudflare's Dynamic Worker Loader (open beta since March 2026) instantiates a sandboxed Worker from runtime-supplied code with `globalOutbound: null` to block all network, at a reported **$0.002 per unique Worker per day** after beta, while Cloudflare itself notes isolates have "a more complex attack surface than hardware virtual machines" ([InfoQ](https://infoq.com/news/2026/04/cloudflare-dynamic-workers-beta); [cogley.jp](https://cogley.jp/articles/cloudflare-dynamic-workers)); a critical sandbox escape in isolated-vm, used by several AI agent frameworks, was patched in August 2026 ([CSO Online](https://csoonline.com/article/4212151/critical-flaw-patched-in-popular-javascript-sandbox-used-in-ai-projects.html)). Pin versions, keep the generated room logic behind a fixed Colyseus shell, and treat per-room headless Chromium as the slower but identically sandboxed fallback.

Platform policy shapes publishing and export. Steam requires a per-game disclosure split into pre-generated content, reviewed like any other content, and live-generated content, which needs described guardrails and a player report path, with failure to disclose resulting in removal ([Steamworks](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/gettingstarted/contentsurvey)); a January 2026 rewrite narrowed scope to player-facing content (art, audio, dialogue, localisation, store assets) and exempted development tooling such as code assistants ([Notebookcheck](https://www.notebookcheck.net/Steam-updates-AI-disclosure-form-requiring-developers-to-report-visible-and-in-game-AI-but-not-background-tools.1206103.0.html)). Roughly **7,818 of ~114,000 Steam games** carried disclosures by July 2025, about one in five new releases, with ~60% of disclosed use being visual assets ([Gigazine](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20250717-steam-games-that-use-generative-ai)). itch.io has required an AI disclosure since November 2024 and de-indexes untagged AI assets ([GamingOnLinux](https://gamingonlinux.com/2024/11/itchio-store-now-requires-ai-generated-content-disclosures-for-assets)); Google Play treats a UGC app hosting AI content as requiring in-app reporting, blocking and proportionate moderation ([Google Play](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9876937?hl=en)); Apple's guideline 4.7 makes a host app responsible for every HTML5 mini-game it serves ([MacStories](https://www.macstories.net/news/apple-amends-app-review-guidelines-to-permit-game-emulators-and-make-other-changes/)). An export feature therefore has the user publish under their own Steamworks account while the platform emits a pre-filled disclosure from the provenance log, ships that provenance inside the build, flags any runtime LLM call as live-generated content, and runs the IP screen at export time. Ownership follows from the Copyright Office position: because purely prompted output may have no copyright owner, contractual licensing rather than ownership has to carry the remix economy. Roblox lets creators retain copyright while taking a perpetual, irrevocable, sublicensable licence and pays out at **$0.0038 per earned Robux** ([Roblox ToU](https://en.help.roblox.com/hc/en-us/articles/115004647846-Roblox-Terms-of-Use); [Roblox DevEx](https://create.roblox.com/docs/production/monetization/developer-exchange)); Epic pays 40% of net Item Shop revenue into an engagement pool weighted toward new and returning players ([Fortnite](https://www.fortnite.com/news/introducing-the-creator-economy-2-0?lang=en-US)); Rosebud's terms, per a search-index excerpt that should be verified against the live page, assert that Rosebud owns prompt-generated material and take a perpetual training licence while its marketing says "you own what you make" ([Rosebud ToS](https://rosebud.ai/terms-of-service)). The clean pattern is that users keep whatever copyright exists in their human contributions, the platform takes a non-exclusive licence sufficient to host and remix, every published game carries a platform remix licence with a recorded attribution chain and optional revenue split, and training use has an explicit opt-out.

Cost is tractable if the templates do their job. Current Claude list prices per million tokens are $4 in / $20 out for Opus 5.5, $2 / $10 for Sonnet 5.5, $0.10 / $0.50 for Haiku 5.5 at prompts under 100K, with cache reads at roughly 5% of input and batch processing at half price ([Claude pricing](https://claude.com/pricing)). A published coding-agent benchmark puts a single Claude Code task at about **$3.14 and 12 minutes at Opus 5 medium effort** (7.9M tokens, mostly cached reads) and $8.23 and 24 minutes at the highest effort ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/agents/coding-agents/comparisons/claude-code-vs-codex)); agents use about four times the tokens of chat and multi-agent systems about fifteen times ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)). A pipeline of six to ten agentic tasks (GDD, scaffold configuration, three to five implementation tasks, level generation, polish) therefore lands around **$20–30 with Opus everywhere** and plausibly under **$10** when the GDD and architecture go to Opus, implementation and review to Sonnet, and edit routing, log triage and rubric scoring to Haiku, with cache-stable prefixes and batch API for offline asset prompts and judge passes. Asset generation adds a few dollars per game at the per-image and per-song prices above (a hundred grid-native sprites and tiles at $0.015–0.18 each, one or two tracks at $0.08). Parameter and content edits that skip code generation should take one to three minutes; full regeneration takes tens of minutes unless stages run in parallel. Every product in the market manages this with credits or token caps (Playground's weekly allowance, Bitmagic's $1,000 creator credits, Rosebud's tiers), and the harness should enforce `--max-turns` and `--max-budget-usd` per game ([best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)). The figures are extrapolations from coding benchmarks, not measurements on game generation, and should be replaced by telemetry within the first month of operation.

## Recommended architecture and phased build plan

The architecture that follows from the evidence is a fixed runtime, a typed template library, a style contract, and a gate at every stage, with LLMs confined to design, selection, authoring and advisory critique.

| Layer | Decision | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Runtime | Phaser 4.x pinned minor, TypeScript, Arcade physics default, Matter or planck optional, wrapped in a platform SDK (fixed scene template, fixed-timestep injectable clock, seeded PRNG, event bus, test seam) | 344 KB gzip, headless, shipped agent skills, Rosebud precedent; the SDK supplies determinism and surface-area control |
| Hosting | each game a self-contained bundle in a sandboxed opaque-origin iframe with first-position meta CSP and a MessageChannel bridge; shared cached engine file | security and cheap remixing in one design |
| Design layer | intake interview, `concept.md`, `gdd.md` with pillars and scope budget, `systems.json`, `style.json` | spec-first workflow; every later gate reads these files |
| Template library | typed, pre-tested systems with unit tests and feel presets as data (Celeste-class platformer, melee hitstop and trauma shake, top-down, survivors arena, deckbuilder) | AutoUE's "predefined patterns"; feel is a default, not a discovery |
| Style resolver | curated catalog of games mapped to unprotectable style cards; LLM extraction with blocked-attribute filter for unknown titles; card shown and editable; named title never leaves the private prompt | Tetris/Spry Fox expression line; vendor norm of blocking characters while honouring style; Copyright Office human-control test |
| Asset pipeline | grid-native generators (PixelLab, Retro Diffusion) for pixel families, reference-conditioned general models one pose per call for painted families, deterministic de-AI pass, code-drawn UI and VFX, OFL/CC0 fonts, ChipTone or ElevenLabs SFX, Stable Audio or Lyria music, per-asset provenance | consistency invariants are machine-checkable; licensing passes through to users |
| Verification | static checks on every edit, template unit tests, headless seeded smoke on Stop, A*/BFS level validator, scripted and coverage bots, screenshot regression with masks, juice linter, budgets, bounded VLM critique with a validated rubric, reviewer subagent against the GDD | rules first, visual second, judge last; "only improving revisions" |
| Multiplayer | Colyseus 0.18 authoritative room template with the SDK prediction layer; shared sim module runs on server via phaser-on-nodejs; generated room logic in Dynamic Workers or isolated-vm behind a fixed shell | tolerates non-deterministic generated clients; no model-written netcode |
| Community | automated gates as pre-listing filter; per-user engagement ranking with featured traffic excluded; remix lineage and depth; one-tag post-play ratings; rewarded test queue; strikes and licence-or-remove for IP | Roblox's documented ranking plus Supergiant's feedback discipline |
| Cost control | Opus for design, Sonnet for implementation and review, Haiku for routing and triage; cache-stable prefixes; batch API offline; per-game turn and dollar caps; edit router avoids codegen where possible | $20–30 to under $10 per game |

The build plan sequences this so that each phase produces a gate the next phase depends on, and so that the platform never ships a genre whose feel and validation are unproven.

| Phase | Timing | Deliverables | Exit criterion |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Foundation | weeks 1–4 | Engine evaluation (same prompt set across Phaser, KAPLAY, Pixi+ECS scored by Playwright pass rate); platform SDK with fixed loop, seeded RNG, event bus and test seam; sandboxed iframe host with CSP and bridge; CLAUDE.md, rules, hooks skeleton; cost telemetry | a hand-written sample game boots headless under Playwright with `__TEST__.ready`, zero console errors and a passing Stop hook |
| 1. Vertical slice | months 2–3 | Precision-platformer and survivors-arena templates with Celeste-class and melee feel presets as data; GDD and systems-select skills; scaffold generator; A*/BFS level validator; scripted bots; juice linter; screenshot regression; budgets; reviewer subagent; code-drawn UI kit and ChipTone audio | 20 representative prompts produce games that pass every deterministic gate unattended at a measured cost per game; human playtest panel rates feel at parity with a hand-tuned reference |
| 2. Style and assets | months 3–6 | Style card schema and editable UI; curated reference catalog (first few hundred titles) with blocked-attribute filter and LLM fallback; PixelLab and Retro Diffusion integration; de-AI pass and asset QA; reference-conditioned painted pipeline; Stable Audio or Lyria music; provenance log; output IP screen; VLM rubric validated on 50–80 labelled screenshots; edit router and checkpoints; publish and remix with lineage | games generated from a named reference are visibly on-style, pass the IP screen, and a blind panel cannot distinguish template assets from generated ones at the pixel-density and palette level |
| 3. Community loop | months 6–9 | Per-user engagement ranking with featured-traffic exclusion; one-tag ratings; rewarded test queue with reliability weighting; stuck heatmaps; DMCA agent, strike system, licence-or-remove flow; jams; judge recalibration from tester labels; top-down life-sim and deckbuilder templates | ranking correlates with D1/D7 return; broken games never enter the feed; tester labels improve judge agreement measurably |
| 4. Multiplayer and export | months 9–12 | Colyseus room template with prediction layer; server-side sim in Dynamic Workers or isolated-vm behind a fixed shell; co-op variants of the arena and platformer templates; Steam and itch.io export with pre-filled disclosure and provenance; licensed style packs if rights-holders sign; action-roguelike template last; distillation of repetitive content (barks, item text) to small models | a generated co-op game survives packet loss and a cheating client in automated tests; an exported build passes Steam's disclosure requirements without manual editing |

Three risks deserve explicit tracking. The reference catalog and style-card design have no documented precedent, so Phase 2 should A/B the card against a raw reference image for consistency before committing to catalog scale. Vendor pricing and terms for PixelLab, Retro Diffusion, ElevenLabs Music and Lyria are inconsistent across sources and must be confirmed in writing before they become a cost model. And the claim that a VLM critic improves outcomes for 2D games has no published validation, which is why it is advisory in every phase.

## Conclusion

The most useful reframing from this research is that the reference games prove quality is a property of constraints rather than of tools, and constraints are precisely what an automated harness can enforce better than a human team can. A solo developer needs discipline to keep one palette and one pixel density for seven years; a pipeline needs a validator. Celeste's forgiveness constants, Shovel Knight's self-imposed NES rules and Dead Cells' single-rig pipeline are all, in harness terms, defaults and lints. That shifts the investment away from the code generator, which every competitor already has and which the research shows is adequate when grounded in engine documentation and templates, and toward the parts no competitor has shown: solver-validated levels, a mandatory test seam, feedback-channel audits, regression gates that admit only improving revisions, and a community ranking that cannot be gamed by featuring.

The second implication is that the platform's legal and quality strategies are the same artifact. A visible, editable style card simultaneously keeps generated output on the idea side of the idea–expression line, documents the human control the Copyright Office requires for authorship, feeds the asset prompts a stable cacheable prefix, and generates the rubric the critic judges against. Provenance logging similarly serves Steam's disclosure form, vendor licence pass-through and the remix lineage that community ranking rewards. Building those two artifacts first, before any genre beyond the platformer and the arena, is what separates a platform that can plausibly produce Celeste-class feel and Dead Cells-class cohesion from one that produces a hosted page that looks finished in a screenshot.
